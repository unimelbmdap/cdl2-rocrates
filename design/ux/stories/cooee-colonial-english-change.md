# How did the way colonists wrote change as they stopped being British and started being Australian?

**Collection:** A COrpus of Oz Early English (COOEE) | **Discipline:** Historical linguistics

Margot, a historical linguist studying the emergence of Australian English in written records, comes to the COOEE collection with a deceptively simple question: when did people writing in colonial Australia start sounding less like Londoners and more like Australians? The corpus spans the entire colonial period from 1788 to 1900, divided into four quarter-century windows, with texts stratified across four registers -- private written, public written, government English, and speech-based. *Can I see all the texts from the earliest period, 1788 to 1825, alongside those from the final period, 1876 to 1900, and compare who was writing in each?* She begins browsing and immediately notices a shift in composition. The earliest period holds 265 texts, many authored by officials like Arthur Philip -- the colony's first Governor, born in London in 1738 -- writing imperial correspondence and government dispatches back to England with zero years lived in Australia. By the fourth period, 419 texts appear, dominated by private correspondence from families like the Maxwells: Hugh, John, James, and William, contributing over 150 letters between them, all classified as private written. The colony's written voice has moved from the governor's desk to the kitchen table.

What catches Margot's attention is how the metadata tracks each author's relationship to Australia at the moment of writing. Each person entity records their birth place, arrival date, and years lived in the colony when they produced each text. *I wonder if I can follow a single writer across decades and watch their language situation change?* Henry Parkes, born in England in 1815, arrived in 1839, and appears in the corpus across three periods. His earliest text is a private letter from 1840, after just one year in New South Wales. By 1867, with twenty-eight years in the colony, he is giving speeches. By 1891, after fifty-two years, he appears in both speech records and government minutes -- the same man, the same birthplace, but a profoundly different linguistic context. Eliza Brown offers another thread: born in England in 1810, she arrived in Western Australia and wrote private letters from 1841 through 1852, her years-in-Australia count ticking upward from zero to eleven across seventeen surviving texts. *Can I gather all the writers who arrived from England and sort their texts by how long they had lived in the colony, regardless of which period the text falls in?* That would let Margot test whether time-in-colony matters more than calendar date for particular linguistic features.

The register dimension opens another line of questioning. Private correspondence accounts for 607 of the 1,354 texts and swells dramatically in the final period -- from 99 texts in the first quarter-century to 258 in the last. Public written material peaks in the second period at 145 texts, then subsides. Government English holds relatively steady across all four periods, at around 50 texts each. Speech-based records, transcribed from court proceedings, political speeches, and parliamentary debates, remain the smallest category throughout. *Can I compare the private letters written from New South Wales, which dominates the collection with 649 texts, against those from the newer colonies like Victoria, Western Australia, and Queensland, to see whether the frontier states show different patterns?* Victoria alone contributes 386 texts, mostly from the gold-rush era onward, while Western Australia accounts for 115, and Queensland just 44. The geographic unevenness is itself a finding -- the older, more established colonies generated more surviving written material, but the question is whether their English also diverged.

The social class annotations add a further layer. Each author is tagged with a social class from Upper Class down to Lower Class, and the recipients of private letters carry their own class and location tags -- many addressed to family back in England. Margot notices that early-period private letters are overwhelmingly sent from upper-middle-class writers to recipients in England, while later-period letters increasingly travel between people already in Australia. The corpus also records text types with fine granularity: 534 texts are private correspondence, 156 are newspaper articles and broadsides, 110 are imperial correspondence, 96 are parliamentary minutes, and smaller counts cover diaries, memoirs, legal documents, petitions, speeches, verse, and even five plays. *Could I trace how the language of petitions and proclamations shifts across the four periods, compared with the relative stability of legal English, to see which registers were most permeable to local innovation?* That comparison -- formal government prose resisting change while newspapers and private letters absorb it -- would map the social channels through which Australian English actually spread. Margot saves the collection and begins planning her concordance searches, knowing that the metadata alone has already revealed the structural story of who was writing, to whom, from where, and how long they had called Australia home.
