# What did Holmer actually record, and where do the gaps start?

**Collection:** Holmer Fieldnotes | **Discipline:** Language documentation / Indigenous language studies

Tara, a language documentation researcher based in Brisbane, has been asked to prepare a comparative study of Wakka Wakka and Gubbi Gubbi wordlists. She knows that Nils Holmer, a Swedish linguist, spent the mid-1960s to 1970s filling notebooks with wordlists, songs, and stories from Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities across Queensland and New South Wales, but she has never seen the unpublished fieldnotes themselves. Before committing to months of archival work, she needs a sense of what the collection actually contains. *How many languages did Holmer document, and which ones? Can I see everything in this collection at a glance -- which notebooks cover which languages, and where they were recorded?* She browses the collection and finds roughly thirty language groups represented, from Wakka Wakka and Gubbi Gubbi on the Wide Bay coast to Kuku Yalanji and Guugu Yimidhirr in the far north, and even the Torres Strait languages Kala Lagaw Ya and Meriam Mir. Some are familiar names; others, like Nunagal and Dunggija, appear without standard reference codes, immediately raising a question. *Are those language names that Holmer used in the field but that don't match modern Austlang or Glottolog identifiers, and what do I do with them?*

Tara notices the twenty-five notebooks are grouped into series: seven Warrego-Burdekin volumes, eight Wakka volumes, three Northern volumes, and a handful of standalone notebooks with names like "KokaJallandji," "GalaliPoonthamaraWestern," and "SongsAndNarratives1973." Some notebook titles mention specific places -- Cherbourg appears twice, which makes sense as a major site where speakers of many displaced language groups lived together. *I wonder if the Cherbourg notebooks contain material from languages whose traditional Country is hundreds of kilometres away, like Kullilli or Margany from the western plains?* That overlap between place of recording and language of origin matters for her comparative work: a wordlist elicited at Cherbourg from a Gangulu speaker is a different sociolinguistic situation from one recorded on Gangulu Country near Emerald.

The geographic coordinates attached to each language entry draw her attention next. The collection stretches from the Torres Strait islands at roughly 10 degrees south all the way down to Gamilaraay Country near the New South Wales border at nearly 30 degrees south, with inland reach as far west as Wakaya Country in the Northern Territory. *Can I see all these languages laid out on a map, so I can tell whether Holmer's coverage follows a route -- perhaps moving along the coast and then inland -- or whether there are whole regions he skipped?* She can already see a concentration in south-east Queensland: Wakka Wakka, Gubbi Gubbi, Gooreng Gooreng, Butchulla, Bundjalung, and Gidhabal are all within a few degrees of each other. The western languages -- Punthamara, Kullilli, Bidjara -- form a second cluster around the Channel Country. The far-northern languages, Kuku Yalanji and Guugu Yimidhirr, sit apart. Understanding these spatial patterns will help Tara decide whether Holmer's data is dense enough in any one region to support the kind of systematic comparison she has in mind.

She also notices the alternative names listed for each language -- Wakka Wakka alone has over forty recorded variants, from "Wacca Wacca" to "Woka Woka." These historical spellings are exactly the kind of thing that trips up cross-referencing with other archives. The Glottolog and Austlang codes, where present, give her a way to link Holmer's data to modern reference databases, but the two languages without codes will need manual investigation. The collection's temporal coverage, mid-1960s to 1970s, also positions it at a critical moment: after significant language shift but before many modern documentation projects began. *Could these notebooks preserve vocabulary or song texts for languages where no other mid-century source exists?* That question alone justifies the deeper dive. Tara bookmarks the collection and begins drafting her fieldwork plan, knowing she will need to return to each notebook series in turn, but now armed with a clear picture of what Holmer's unpublished work actually spans.
