# How did children move through Victoria's care system, and where did the records end up?

**Collection:** WAMI -- Pathways (Who Am I? Project) | **Discipline:** Welfare / institutional history

Helen, a welfare historian researching the institutional care system in Victoria, has spent years piecing together how children were moved between homes, missions, and government departments from the mid-nineteenth century onward. She knows from published inquiries -- the 2004 Forgotten Australians report, the Inquiry into Children in Institutional Care -- that the system was vast and fragmented, but the secondary literature rarely captures how individual organisations were connected to one another, which bodies ran which homes, and what happened to the records when an institution closed or changed its name. The Pathways collection, compiled by the Who Am I? Project, promises something different: over twenty-four thousand entities documenting care organisations, the places they operated, the legislation that governed them, and the archival resources that survive. *Can I see the full landscape of care organisations in Victoria -- not just the well-known ones, but the small cottage homes, the reception centres, the babies' homes that have disappeared from public memory?* Browsing through the five hundred and sixty-six organisations, she finds names she recognises -- the Salvation Army, Berry Street, MacKillop Family Services -- alongside dozens she does not: the Arms of Jesus Babies' Home, Kedesh Maternity Home, Acheron Youth Training Centre, Lyndon Lodge. Each entry carries a function label -- Care Provider, Adoption Agency, Community Service Organisation -- and dates of operation, some stretching back to the 1850s. The sheer number of institutions that existed to house children who were separated from their families is itself a finding.

What draws Helen deeper is the web of succession that connects these organisations across time. She selects the Mission of St James and St John, an Anglican city mission established in 1919 that ran homes for children, unmarried mothers, and families in need for nearly eighty years. The record reveals that this single mission operated more than twenty separate institutions: the Andrew Kerr Memorial Home, the Arms of Jesus Babies' Home, Blackburn South Cottages, St Luke's Toddlers' Home, Kedesh Maternity Home, and many others. Canon Lamble, the Mission's leader from 1925 to 1939, had envisioned "a chain of Mission homes" -- the unmarried mother cared for at Kedesh, her baby at the Arms of Jesus, the toddler at St Luke's, and the school-age child at the Glenroy Homes. *I wonder if I can trace that chain -- can I follow the path a child might have taken through these connected institutions, and see which ones fed into which?* The "Run by" relationships make this visible: each home is linked back to the Mission, and the Mission itself is linked forward to Anglicare Victoria, the organisation formed in 1997 when the Mission merged with the Mission to the Streets and Lanes and St John's Home for Boys and Girls. A child who passed through Blackburn South Cottages in the 1960s was in the care of an institution whose records might now sit with an organisation that did not exist until three decades later.

That question of records custody is central to Helen's research. She notices that the collection tracks not only which organisations ran which homes, but which bodies now manage the surviving records. Berry Street's Heritage and Background Information Service manages records from Berry Street itself and from Sutherland Homes for Children, its predecessor. The Past Residents Support Service at Child and Family Services Ballarat holds records from the Ballarat District Orphan Asylum, established in 1865, and from the Ballarat Orphanage that succeeded it. MacKillop Family Services, created in 1997 to continue the work of the Sisters of Mercy, the Christian Brothers, and the Sisters of St Joseph, inherited records from all three religious orders. *Can I see all the organisations whose records are now managed by a single body -- and from that, understand how many predecessor institutions a care leaver would need to navigate to find their own file?* The ninety-three "Manages records of" relationships in the collection begin to answer this, mapping a custody chain that is as tangled as the institutional history itself. For someone who grew up in a home run by the Christian Brothers in the 1950s, the path to their personal records now runs through MacKillop Family Services -- but only if they know that the Christian Brothers' child care work became part of MacKillop, and only if they know what the home was called at the time they were there. Organisations changed names frequently: Glastonbury Children's Home became Glastonbury Child and Family Services in 1984; Wesley Central Parish Mission became Wesley Mission Melbourne in 2001. The "Also known as" and "Previous/Subsequent" relationships -- over a hundred and twenty succession links and seventeen acronyms -- capture exactly the kind of name-shifting that makes personal record searches so difficult.

The legislation woven through the collection adds a structural dimension that Helen had not expected to find so richly documented. The Neglected Children's Act of 1887 made it mandatory to commit a child to state care if they were found living in a brothel, gave private persons the power to remove children from such situations, and ensured all neglected children became wards of the state. This single piece of legislation is connected to the Department for Neglected Children, to the Salvation Army, to St Joseph's Home for Destitute Children, to child rescuer Selina Sutherland, and to the glossary term "State Ward." *Can I follow a piece of legislation to every organisation and person it affected -- and then follow those organisations forward to see where they led?* The Social Welfare Act of 1960 connects to the Social Welfare Branch and its successor the Social Welfare Department, which in turn connects to the Department of Community Welfare Services. Each legislative change created new departmental structures, renamed old ones, and shifted the terms under which children could be placed in care. The thirty-two pieces of legislation in the collection, from the Neglected and Criminal Children's Act of 1864 to the Victorian Freedom of Information Act of 1982, form a timeline of how the state's relationship with vulnerable children was defined and redefined over more than a century. For Helen, the ability to move between a piece of legislation, the government departments it created, the homes those departments licensed, and the archival resources that survive from those homes is not an abstract convenience. It is the difference between understanding the care system as a set of isolated institutions and seeing it as a single, shifting, interconnected apparatus -- one whose consequences are still being traced by the people who passed through it.
