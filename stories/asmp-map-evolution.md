# How did Arrowsmith's map of Western Australia grow and change as the colony itself took shape?

**Collection:** ASMP -- Arrowsmith's Australian Maps | **Discipline:** Cartographic history

Felix, a cartographic historian researching how nineteenth-century commercial mapmakers responded to the flood of new geographic knowledge from colonial Australia, turns to the Arrowsmith collection with a question about one particular sequence of maps. The ASMP catalogue, compiled by Dorothy Prescott, documents 19,146 entities tracing John Arrowsmith's Australian cartographic output from his London workshops between the 1830s and 1860s. The collection organises maps into summaries, which describe an entire plate across its lifetime, and states, which capture each individual reissue with its specific changes. These states are threaded together by previous and subsequent links that form chronological chains -- a kind of genealogy of revision. *Can I follow one map title from its first printing through every reissue and see exactly what changed each time?* Felix begins with the "Discoveries in Western Australia," first issued on 31 May 1833 as plate 36 in Arrowsmith's London Atlas, dedicated to R.W. Hay, an Under Secretary of State for the Colonies. The summary note records seven states across six years, but also something curious: in all that time, Arrowsmith never changed a single piece of internal information on the map. Every state is merely a reissue, with only the imprint updated. The map shows the extreme south-west of the continent south of Perth at a scale of almost 1:1,000,000, with five inset town plans for Guildford, Augusta, Kelmscott, Perth, and Fremantle. But the plate stayed frozen, as if Arrowsmith lost interest.

That frozen plate is exactly what draws Felix deeper. The previous-subsequent chain for this map runs from "Discoveries in Western Australia, 1833/1" through seven states to "1833/7 (1838/2)" -- and then something remarkable happens. The chain does not end; it crosses into an entirely different map title. The next link leads to "The Colony of Western Australia, 1839/1," a successor plate that took the same plate number, 36, but was engraved on a larger copper plate, extending the coverage northward by adjusting the latitude from 31 degrees south to 30 degrees 57 minutes south and adding eight new counties north of Perth. *I wonder if I can see everything that connects to the original Discoveries plate -- the archival records, the people whose survey work it drew on, the replacement map it gave rise to?* The collection records that Alexander Collie, a naval surgeon born in 1793, and William Preston, a Royal Navy lieutenant born in 1803, both appear across every single state of the Discoveries map and then carry over into every state of its successor, the Colony plate. Their presence on both maps tells Felix that Arrowsmith was drawing on the same pool of survey data from the earliest days of the Swan River settlement, even as his cartographic ambitions outgrew the original plate.

The Colony of Western Australia itself ran through eight states from 1839 to 1863, and here the chain reveals the physical constraints of atlas mapmaking. In its later states, when Arrowsmith needed to record John Septimus Roe's 1848-49 explorations along the south coast, the original plate could no longer contain the information. Rather than start a new map, Arrowsmith commissioned an extension plate that added 36 centimetres of width, pushing the coverage eastward from 120 to nearly 124 degrees east longitude. The final state was issued from Arrowsmith's last address at 35 Hereford Square, South Kensington, and the summary note records that it was printed in an unfinished condition -- the right-hand and top neat-lines were never drawn, and the longitudinal values along the top are missing entirely. *Can I lay out the chain of states side by side to see when each burst of new information arrived -- and when the plate simply sat unchanged between reissues?* That pattern of revision and dormancy would reveal not just Arrowsmith's workflow but the rhythm of colonial exploration itself: long silences punctuated by expeditions that flooded London with new data.

Felix notices the same pattern repeating across the collection's other map families. The "Map of the Discoveries in Australia," plate 35, lasted just three years and five states before Arrowsmith abandoned it in favour of the "South Eastern Portion of Australia" at a larger scale of 1:2,660,000, which then survived twenty years and twelve states from 1838 to 1858, accumulating the routes of Mitchell, Sturt, and Eyre and eventually marking the gold fields of New South Wales in its parliamentary issue. "Van Diemens Land" went through six states between 1832 and 1842 without a single change to its internal content -- Arrowsmith recognised that the Surveyor General in Hobart could produce better maps than himself. Each archival resource in the collection -- over 300 in total, held at institutions like the National Library of Australia -- links to a specific map state through a primary relationship, meaning Felix can trace the provenance of individual surviving copies. *Could I find every copy of a particular state held in different archives, to understand how widely a given printing was distributed?* That question connects cartographic history to the history of collecting itself, and the ASMP's structure of summaries, states, archival resources, and temporal chains makes it possible to ask it without ever losing the thread of which map came first, what replaced it, and why.
