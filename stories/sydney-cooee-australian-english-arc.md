# What does Australian English look like when you can hold colonial letters and modern interviews side by side?

**Collection:** Sydney Speaks + A COrpus of Oz Early English (COOEE) | **Discipline:** Sociolinguistics

Tessa, a sociolinguist investigating the long arc of Australian English, has always been frustrated by the gap between the written record and the spoken one. Colonial-era evidence is entirely textual -- letters, government dispatches, newspaper columns -- while the best modern evidence comes from recorded speech. These two kinds of data sit in different archives, organised by different logics, and rarely speak to each other. When she discovers that both Sydney Speaks and COOEE are available as structured collections, each with detailed person-level metadata, she wonders whether she can bridge that gap. *Can I lay these two collections alongside each other and see the full timeline of Australian English from 1788 to the 2020s?* COOEE covers the colonial written record from First Fleet correspondence through to Federation, stratified into four quarter-century periods and four registers -- Private Written, Public Written, Government English, and Speech Based. Sydney Speaks picks up in the twentieth century with sociolinguistic interviews: the Bicentennial Oral History Project captured speakers born around 1900, the Sydney Social Dialect Survey recorded Anglo-Celtic, Greek, and Italian Sydneysiders in the late 1970s, and the ANU Corpus of Sydney Speech collected interviews with adults and young adults from 2015 to 2022. Between them, the two collections span over two hundred years and hold more than three and a half thousand texts and recordings.

What strikes Tessa first is how differently the two collections represent their people. COOEE tracks each author's birth place, arrival date, and years lived in Australia at the time of writing -- Arthur Philip, born in London in 1738, arriving with the First Fleet, contributing government correspondence with zero years in the colony. By the 1890s, writers born in New South Wales appear regularly: William Charles Wentworth, born in the colony in 1790, is among the earliest Australian-born voices in the written record. Sydney Speaks, by contrast, records each speaker's ethnicity, age group, home suburb, occupation, and level of education. Mike, born in 1960, Anglo background, living in North Epping, working as a storeperson, educated to Year 12, recorded for the Sydney Social Dialect Survey in 1978. Dominic, born in 1964, Italian background, in Killarney Heights. Patrick, born in 1966, Greek background, in Earlwood. *I wonder if I can match the social categories across these two collections -- do the "birth place" and "years lived in Australia" fields in COOEE map onto the "ethnicity" categories in Sydney Speaks, so that I can trace the shift from British-born writers to Australian-born speakers of diverse backgrounds?* COOEE's 919 person entries with birth information show that the largest group of colonial writers were born in England, followed by those from Northern Ireland and Scotland, with 165 born in New South Wales itself. Sydney Speaks' 251 speakers with birth dates are overwhelmingly Australian-born, split across Anglo, Italian, Greek, and Cantonese Chinese backgrounds. The transition from a transplanted English to a locally rooted, multilingual Australian English sits right in the gap between these two collections.

The register and genre information offers another seam to mine. COOEE's four registers -- Private Written, Public Written, Government English, and Speech Based -- distinguish between a convict's letter home and a proclamation from the Governor's office. The Speech Based register, the smallest in COOEE, contains transcribed court proceedings, parliamentary debates, and political speeches: the closest the colonial record comes to capturing how people actually talked. *Can I pull out everything tagged as Speech Based in COOEE and compare it with the earliest Sydney Speaks interviews -- the Bicentennial Oral History recordings of people born around 1900 -- to see what connects the last representations of colonial speech to the first recordings of twentieth-century Australian voices?* Those Bicentennial interviews were conducted in 1987 and 1988 with elderly speakers who had lived through two world wars; their parents and grandparents would have been contemporaries of the COOEE fourth-period writers. The overlap is not exact, but it is real. A speech transcribed in an 1890s courtroom and a recorded interview with someone born in 1895 are separated by medium and method, but they are listening to the same generation.

The geographic dimension sharpens the comparison further. COOEE draws on texts produced across New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia, the Northern Territory, Tasmania, and even Norfolk Island and New Zealand -- twenty-eight distinct place entities in all. Sydney Speaks narrows the lens to a single city, with speakers located in suburbs across metropolitan Sydney: North Epping, Killarney Heights, Earlwood. *Could I filter COOEE down to texts written in New South Wales -- which already dominates the collection -- and then compare those colonial New South Wales voices with Sydney Speaks' modern Sydney ones, holding geography roughly constant while letting time vary?* That would control for the regional dimension and make the diachronic comparison tighter. The question Tessa keeps circling back to is not whether Australian English changed -- that is obvious -- but whether the structured metadata in these two collections can reveal the social channels through which it changed: from formal government prose by British-born officials, through private letters by settlers who had spent decades in the colony, to recorded conversations with the grandchildren and great-grandchildren of those settlers, now joined by speakers from Italian, Greek, and Chinese families who arrived in the twentieth century. The long arc is not just a timeline. It is a story about who gets to count as an Australian English speaker, and what kind of evidence survives to prove it.
